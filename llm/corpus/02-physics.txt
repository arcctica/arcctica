gravity is the way mass bends space and time. newton described it as a force that pulls objects toward each other, and that works well for falling apples and orbiting planets. einstein described it as geometry: a heavy object curves the space and time around it, and everything near it, even light, follows the straightest possible path through that curved space.

a planet stays in orbit around the sun because it is always falling toward the sun while moving sideways so fast that it keeps missing. if the planet moved more slowly, it would spiral inward. if it moved faster, it would drift away. the speed of a circular orbit depends only on the mass of the star and on the distance, and it gets smaller as the distance grows.

light has no mass, yet gravity bends its path. in nineteen nineteen astronomers photographed stars close to the sun during a total eclipse and found that the stars looked slightly shifted from their normal places. the sun had bent the light on its way to the earth. that measurement made einstein famous, because his theory predicted the size of the shift and the old theory did not.

for every mass there is a radius so small that, if you squeeze all of the mass inside it, nothing can escape, and a black hole forms. this radius is called the schwarzschild radius. for the sun it is about three kilometers. for the earth it is less than one centimeter. a black hole is not a cosmic vacuum cleaner. far away from it, gravity is the same as the gravity of any other object with the same mass.

close to a black hole there are some special distances. at one and a half times the schwarzschild radius, light can travel around the hole in a circle, but the circle is unstable, and the smallest push sends the light inward or outward. this place is called the photon sphere. at three times the schwarzschild radius is the innermost stable orbit. matter can circle the hole safely outside of it, and inside of it matter has to plunge in.

the gas that falls toward a black hole almost never falls straight in. it carries some sideways motion, so it forms a flat spinning disk called an accretion disk. the gas in the disk rubs against itself, heats up to millions of degrees, and glows. the inner part of the disk is the hottest and the brightest, and the glow gets weaker farther away from the hole.

the disk spins, and the spinning changes how it looks. the side of the disk that moves toward you looks brighter and bluer, and the side that moves away from you looks dimmer and redder. this is the doppler effect, the same effect that changes the sound of a passing ambulance. at speeds close to the speed of light the brightening is strong, and it makes one side of the disk clearly brighter.

light that climbs out of a gravitational field loses energy, and its color shifts toward red. this is called gravitational redshift. time also runs slower deeper inside a gravitational field. a clock close to a black hole ticks more slowly than a clock far away, and the difference grows without limit as the clock approaches the horizon.

the picture of a black hole is bent in an unexpected way. light from the back side of the disk travels over the top of the hole and reaches you from above, so you see the far side of the disk lifted into an arch over the shadow. light that goes around the hole once more makes a thinner and fainter copy of the disk, and light that goes around twice makes an even thinner copy. these copies pile up into a bright thin ring around the shadow.

the dark circle in the middle is called the shadow. it is larger than the horizon itself, about two and a half times the schwarzschild radius, because the gravity of the hole bends rays that pass near it and swallows them. in two thousand nineteen the event horizon telescope published the first image of such a shadow, from the center of the galaxy messier eighty seven. to make it, radio dishes in several countries were linked together so that they worked like a single telescope as wide as the earth.

when a distant star lies exactly behind a heavy object, its light bends around the object on every side and forms a ring. this is called an einstein ring. if the alignment is not perfect, the star appears as one or two arcs. the heavier the lens, the larger the ring. in a computer picture the same effect appears for any star that lines up with the hole, and the stars close to the ring are stretched into long thin arcs.

near a black hole the path of a light ray cannot be written with a simple formula, so the computer takes it in small steps. at each step it uses the equation of motion to update the position and the direction. the simplest method moves in a straight line for each step and makes a small error every time. a better method, called runge kutta, looks at the slope several times inside one step and combines the answers, and its error is far smaller for the same step size.

a simulation is only as good as its checks. far away from the black hole the rays must go in straight lines, and a picture that bends them there has a bug. a ray that goes straight toward the center must fall in. the size of the shadow must match the known value. when a simulation passes tests like these, you can trust the strange pictures it makes in the places where nobody can check them.

a person who falls into a large black hole would notice nothing special when crossing the horizon. to a faraway observer, the person would seem to slow down and fade, and they would never be seen to cross. for a small black hole the difference of gravity between the head and the feet is so large that it would stretch a falling person apart long before the horizon. the size of the hole decides which of these stories is true.

stephen hawking showed that black holes are not perfectly black. they very slowly give off a faint glow of particles, and the smaller the black hole, the hotter the glow. for a black hole with the mass of a star the glow is much weaker than the cold background of the universe, so no one has seen it. the idea still matters, because it connects gravity, quantum physics and heat in a single formula.

energy cannot be created or destroyed, and that simple rule gives a lot of power. a stone that falls gains speed because it loses height. a planet on an oval orbit moves fast when it is close to the star and slow when it is far away. a ray of light that falls into a gravity well gains energy, and a ray that climbs out loses it. almost every calculation in physics starts by asking what stays the same while everything else changes.
